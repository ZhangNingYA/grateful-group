### Sleep deprivation and catching up on rest

<SentenceNote number="C01">
  As it is, sleep is so undervalued that getting by on fewer hours has become a badge of honor.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="C02">
  Plus, we live in a culture that caters to the late-nighter, from 24-hour grocery stores to online shopping sites that never close.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="C03">
  It’s no surprise, then, that more than half of American adults don’t get the 7 to 9 hours of shut-eye every night as recommended by sleep experts.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="C04">
  Whether or not we can catch up on sleep—on the weekend, say—is a hotly debated topic among sleep researchers.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="C05">
  The latest evidence suggests that while it isn’t ideal, it might help.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="C06">
  When Liu, the UCLA sleep researcher and professor of medicine, brought chronically sleep-restricted people into the lab for a weekend of sleep during which they logged about 10 hours per night, they showed improvements in the ability of insulin to process blood sugar.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="C07">
  That suggests that catch-up sleep may undo some but not all of the damage that sleep deprivation causes, which is encouraging given how many adults don’t get the hours they need each night.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="C08">
  Still, Liu isn’t ready to endorse the habit of sleeping less and making up for it later.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="C09">
  Sleeping pills, while helpful for some, are not necessarily an effective remedy either.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="C10">
  “A sleeping pill will target one area of the brain, but there’s never going to be a perfect sleeping pill, because you couldn’t really replicate the different chemicals moving in and out of different parts of the brain to go through the different stages of sleep,” says Dr. Nancy Collop, director of the Emory University Sleep Center.
</SentenceNote>

## Reading Passages

### Passage One · Learning how to ask better questions

<SentenceNote number="R1-01">
  More than a decade ago, cognitive scientists John Bransford and Daniel Schwartz, both then at Vanderbilt University, found that what distinguished young adults from children was not the ability to retain facts or apply prior knowledge to a new situation but a quality they called “preparation for future learning.”
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-02">
  The researchers asked fifth graders and college students to create a recovery plan to protect bald eagles from extinction.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-03">
  Shockingly, the two groups came up with plans of similar quality (though the college students had better spelling skills).
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-04">
  From the standpoint of a traditional educator, this outcome indicated that schooling had failed to help students think about ecosystems and extinction, major scientific ideas.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-05">
  The researchers decided to go deeper, however.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-06">
  They asked both groups to generate questions about important issues needed to create recovery plans.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-07">
  On this task, they found large differences.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-08">
  College students focused on critical issues of interdependence between eagles and their habitat.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-09">
  Fifth graders tended to focus on features of individual eagles (“How big are they?” and “What do they eat?”).
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-10">
  The college students had cultivated the ability to ask questions, the cornerstone of critical thinking.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-11">
  They had learned how to learn.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-12">
  Museums and other institutions of informal learning may be better suited to teach this skill than elementary and secondary schools.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-13">
  At the Exploratorium in San Francisco, we recently studied how learning to ask good questions can affect the quality of people’s scientific inquiry.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-14">
  We found that when we taught participants to ask “What if?” and “How can?” questions that nobody present would know the answer to and that would spark exploration, they engaged in better inquiry at the next exhibit—asking more questions, performing more experiments and making better interpretations of their results.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-15">
  Specifically, their questions became more comprehensive at the new exhibit.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-16">
  Rather than merely asking about something they wanted to try, they tended to include both cause and effect in their question.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-17">
  Asking juicy questions appears to be a transferable skill for deepening collaborative inquiry into the science content found in exhibits.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-18">
  This type of learning is not confined to museums or institutional settings.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-19">
  Informal learning environments tolerate failure better than schools.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-20">
  Perhaps many teachers have too little time to allow students to form and pursue their own questions and too much ground to cover in the curriculum.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-21">
  But people must acquire this skill somewhere.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-22">
  Our society depends on them being able to make critical decisions about their own medical treatment, say, or what we must do about global energy needs and demands.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-23">
  For that, we have a robust informal system that gives no grades, takes all comers, and is available even on holidays and weekends.
</SentenceNote>
