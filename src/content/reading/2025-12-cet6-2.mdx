### Passage One · Solidarity and interdependence

<SentenceNote number="R1-01">
  As interdependent beings we cannot thrive independent of others.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-02">
  We like to work with people who are like-minded, with whom we share a common interest, or who also believe in a cause we are serious about.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-03">
  This is evident in how we have organised ourselves into various collective groups such as nations, regions, communities, organisations, and families.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-04">
  Does the group you belong to have a strong collective consciousness or shared beliefs?
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-05">
  Is there interdependence among its members?
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-06">
  Do you consider this group a significant part of your existence?
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-07">
  If your answers are affirmative, then you are experiencing a micro version of solidarity.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-08">
  What is solidarity?
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-09">
  Its simplest definition in the dictionary is:
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-10">
  "a unifying opinion, feeling, purpose or interest among a group of people."
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-11">
  In this increasingly diverse society we live in, solidarity takes on a complex and multifaceted character as it forms bonds of interdependence among unique individuals living in a fast-changing environment and working within a complex division of labour.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-12">
  One facet of solidarity fits well with the principles of communitarianism(社群主义).
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-13">
  In this setting, individuals give up their own interests to serve the common good.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-14">
  However, its strict adherence to social cohesiveness and exclusiveness restricts expression and practice of individual differences and preferences.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-15">
  Consequently, individuality and autonomy is sacrificed to maintain the unity of the group.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-16">
  Solidarity is also viewed as a reaction to the extreme emphasis on individualism and freedom of choice.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-17">
  It goes against the liberal idea of unrestricted individuals who see society as a hindrance to their freedom to make choices and to act according to what is good for them regardless of how these will affect others.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-18">
  But despite its many facets, social theorists have viewed solidarity as a key factor in bringing about order and progress in society.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-19">
  They believe that for human society to survive as it becomes more diverse, more new bonds need to be formed based on the differences.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-20">
  In the light of this, they regard a genuine solidarity as more than just shallow relationships based on common interests.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-21">
  Solidarity is not just a product of strategic calculations of how much it will benefit the self.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-22">
  It is not an exclusive class founded on the you-and-me-against-the-world principle.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-23">
  Rather, it is inclusive and is not forced.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-24">
  Acting out of solidarity means supporting and standing up for each other—in recognition that one's fate is in the fate of the other.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-25">
  It is a bond characterised by cooperative practices and strengthened by the concern for the well-being of others or the common good.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-26">
  It fights for the protection of human dignity and listens to and acts on the cries of the most vulnerable.
</SentenceNote>

### Passage Two · Setting boundaries around unwanted advice

<SentenceNote number="R2-01">
  People who repeatedly give unwanted advice can be well-meaning and genuinely want to help.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-02">
  Others might do this out of a sense of self-worth around the ability to influence others.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-03">
  One study found that people with a high tendency to seek power were more likely to give advice than those with an opposite tendency.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-04">
  Under the guise of unselfishness, people may be driven to give you unwanted advice because it makes them feel powerful or in control.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-05">
  They may not be fully aware of this drive, however.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-06">
  These people may also display a problematic degree of emotional vulnerability, becoming upset very quickly, and taking a long time to calm down.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-07">
  It is possible that their emotions were only validated in childhood when they were at their loudest, encouraging them to adopt responses to discomfort that are excessive in most situations.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-08">
  When someone is giving advice in order to make themselves feel more powerful, there is underlying anxiety to their behavior that recipients of the advice tend to notice.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-09">
  It can be tempting in this situation to react harshly to the advice giver and to accuse them of being manipulative, but this approach might produce an undesirable result.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-10">
  If the act of giving advice is contributing actively to someone's feelings of self-worth, an outright rejection may be perceived as a threat, activating their fight-or-flight response, possibly causing them to double down on their validation-seeking behavior, or leading to a larger conflict.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-11">
  The key is to validate without over-identifying.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-12">
  You can let them know that you have heard them and appreciate where they are coming from without taking on the potentially damaging narrative that you could not have gotten by without their help.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-13">
  You had better say something like, "Thanks for the idea.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-14">
  I have my own plan for handling this, but I really appreciate your perspective and will take it into consideration.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-15">
  Can I let you know when I need help in the future?”
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-16">
  If you have trouble setting boundaries tactfully, prioritize working on your own ability to self-regulate.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-17">
  As uncomfortable as it may make you continuously receiving unwanted advice, if you can respond with compassion, the situation will likely diffuse much faster.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-18">
  Though it is usually not the intended outcome of giving unsolicited advice, many who receive it often feel stressed, offended, or simply annoyed by unwanted suggestions.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-19">
  Setting a boundary in this regard, if you feel you need one, is perfectly reasonable and something that can bring you increased emotional safety.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-20">
  It is best to set boundaries in a way that takes into account the individual's underlying reasons for doing what they are doing to avoid unnecessary conflict and more stress.
</SentenceNote>
