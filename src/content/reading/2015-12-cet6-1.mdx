## Paragraph Matching

### The Impossibility of Rapid Energy Transitions

### Paragraph A

<SentenceNote number="M-A01">
  Politicians are fond of promising rapid energy transitions.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="M-A02">
  Whether it is a transition from imported to domestic oil or from coal-powered electricity production to natural-gas power plants, politicians love to talk big.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="M-A03">
  Unfortunately for them (and often the taxpayers), our energy systems are a bit like an aircraft carrier: they are unbelievably expensive, they are built to last for a very long time, they have a huge amount of inertia (meaning it takes a lot of energy to set them moving), and they have a lot of momentum once they are set in motion.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="M-A04">
  No matter how hard you try, you can’t turn something that large on a dime, or even a few thousand dimes.
</SentenceNote>

### Paragraph B

<SentenceNote number="M-B01">
  In physics, moving objects have two characteristics relevant to understanding the dynamics of energy systems: inertia and momentum.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="M-B02">
  Inertia is the resistance of objects to efforts to change their state of motion.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="M-B03">
  If you try to push a boulder, it pushes you back.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="M-B04">
  Once you have started the boulder rolling, it develops momentum, which is defined by its mass and velocity.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="M-B05">
  Momentum is said to be “conserved,” that is, once you build it up, it has to go somewhere.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="M-B06">
  So a heavy object, like a football player moving at a high speed, has a lot of momentum—that is, once he is moving, it is hard to change his state of motion.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="M-B07">
  If you want to change his course, you have only a few choices: you can stop him, transferring (possibly painfully) some of his kinetic energy to your own body, or you can approach alongside and slowly apply pressure to gradually alter his course.
</SentenceNote>

### Paragraph C

<SentenceNote number="M-C01">
  But there are other kinds of momentum as well.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="M-C02">
  After all, we don’t speak only of objects or people as having momentum; we speak of entire systems having momentum.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="M-C03">
  Whether it’s a sports team or a presidential campaign, everybody relishes having the big momentum, because it makes them harder to stop or change direction.
</SentenceNote>

### Paragraph D

<SentenceNote number="M-D01">
  One kind of momentum is technological momentum.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="M-D02">
  When a technology is deployed, its impacts reach far beyond itself.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="M-D03">
  Consider the incandescent bulb, an object currently hated by many environmentalists and energy-efficiency advocates.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="M-D04">
  The incandescent light bulb, invented by Thomas Edison, which came to be the symbol of inspiration, has been developed into hundreds, if not thousands, of forms.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="M-D05">
  Today, a visit to a lighting store reveals a stunning array of choices.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="M-D06">
  There are standard-shaped bulbs, flame-shaped bulbs, colored globe-shaped bulbs, and more.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="M-D07">
  It is quite easy, with all that choice, to change a light bulb.
</SentenceNote>

### Paragraph E

<SentenceNote number="M-E01">
  But the momentum of incandescent lighting does not stop there.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="M-E02">
  All of those specialized bulbs led to the building of specialized light fixtures, from the desk lamp you study by, to the ugly but beloved hand-painted Chinese lamp you inherited from your grandmother, to the ceiling fixture in your closet, to the light in your oven or refrigerator, and to the light that the dentist points at you.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="M-E03">
  It is easy to change a light bulb, sure, but it is harder to change the bulb and its fixture.
</SentenceNote>

### Paragraph F

<SentenceNote number="M-F01">
  And there is more to the story, because not only are the devices that house incandescent bulbs shaped to their underlying characteristics, but rooms and entire buildings have been designed in accordance with how incandescent lighting reflects off walls and windows.

### Paragraph G

<SentenceNote number="M-G01">
  As lighting expert Howard Brandston points out, “Generally, there are no bad light sources, only bad applications.”
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="M-G02">

</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="M-G03">

</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="M-G04">

</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="M-G05">

</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="M-G06">

</SentenceNote>

### Paragraph I

<SentenceNote number="M-I01">
  Another type of momentum we have to think about when planning for changes in our energy systems is labor-pool momentum.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="M-I02">

## Reading Passages

### Passage One · The language of racial identity

<SentenceNote number="R1-01">
  One hundred years ago, “Colored” was the typical way of referring to Americans of African descent.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-02">
  Twenty years later, it was purposefully dropped to make way for “Negro.”
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-03">
  By the late 1960s, that term was overtaken by “Black.”
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-04">
  And then, at a press conference in Chicago in 1988, Jesse Jackson declared that “African American” was the term to embrace.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-05">
  This one was chosen because it echoed the labels of groups, such as “Italian Americans” and “Irish Americans,” that had already been freed of widespread discrimination.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-06">
  A century’s worth of calculated name changes point to the fact that naming any group is a politically freighted exercise.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-07">
  A 2001 study cataloged all the ways in which the term “Black” carried connotations that were more negative than those of “African American.”
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-08">
  But if it was known that “Black” people were viewed differently from “African Americans,” researchers, until now, hadn’t identified what that gap in perception was derived from.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-09">
  A recent study, conducted by Emory University’s Erika Hall, found that “Black” people are viewed more negatively than “African Americans” because of a perceived difference in socioeconomic status.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-10">
  As a result, “Black” people are thought of as less competent and as having colder personalities.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-11">
  The study’s most striking findings shed light on the racial biases permeating the professional world.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-12">
  Even seemingly harmless details on a resume, it appears, can tap into recruiters’ biases.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-13">
  A job application might mention affiliations with groups such as the “Wisconsin Association of African-American Lawyers” or the “National Black Employees Association,” the names of which apparently have consequences, and are also beyond their members’ control.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-14">
  In one of the study’s experiments, subjects were given a brief description of a man from Chicago with the last name Williams.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-15">
  To one group, he was identified as “African-American,” and another was told he was “Black.”
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-16">
  With little else to go on, they were asked to estimate Mr. Williams’s salary, professional standing, and educational background.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-17">
  The “African-American” group estimated that he earned about \$37,000 a year and had a two-year college degree.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-18">
  The “Black” group, on the other hand, put his salary at about \$29,000, and guessed that he had only “some” college experience.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-19">
  Nearly three-quarters of the first group guessed that Mr. Williams worked at a managerial level, while only 38.5 percent of the second group thought so.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-20">
  Hall’s findings suggest there’s an argument to be made for electing to use “African American,” though one can’t help but get the sense that it’s a decision that papers over the urgency of continued progress.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-21">
  Perhaps a new phrase is needed, one that can bring everyone one big step closer to realizing Du Bois’s original, idealistic hope: “It’s not the name—it’s the Thing that counts.”
</SentenceNote>

### Passage Two · Are colleges preparing students for life after graduation?

<SentenceNote number="R2-01">
  Across the board, American colleges and universities are not doing a very good job of preparing their students for the workplace or their post-graduation lives.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-02">
  This was made clear by the work of two sociologists, Richard Arum and Josipa Roksa.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-03">
  In 2011 they released a landmark study titled “Academically Adrift,” which documented the lack of intellectual growth experienced by many people enrolled in college.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-04">
  In particular, Arum and Roksa found, college students were not developing the critical thinking, analytic reasoning and other higher-level skills that are necessary to thrive in today’s knowledge-based economy and to lead our nation in a time of complex challenges and dynamic change.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-05">
  Arum and Roksa placed the blame for students’ lack of learning on a watered-down college curriculum and lowered undergraduate work standards.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-06">
  Although going to college is supposed to be a full-time job, students spent, on average, only 12 to 14 hours a week studying and many were skating through their semesters without doing a significant amount of reading and writing.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-07">
  Students who take more challenging classes and spend more time studying do learn more.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-08">
  But the priorities of many undergraduates are with extracurricular activities, playing sports, and partying and socializing.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-09">
  Laura Hamilton, the author of a study on parents who pay for college, will argue in a forthcoming book that college administrations are overly concerned with the social and athletic activities of their students.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-10">
  In Paying for the Party, Hamilton describes what she calls the “party pathway,” which eases many students through college, helped along by various clubs that send students into the party scene and a host of easier majors.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-11">
  By sanctioning this watered-down version of college, universities are “catering to the social and educational needs of wealthy students at the expense of others” who won’t enjoy the financial backing or social connections of richer students once they graduate.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-12">
  These students need to build skills and knowledge during college if they are to use their degrees as a stepping-stone to middle-class mobility.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-13">
  But more privileged students must not waste this opportunity either.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-14">
  As recent graduates can testify, the job market isn’t kind to candidates who can’t demonstrate genuine competence, along with a well-cultivated willingness to work hard.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-15">
  Nor is the global economy forgiving of an American workforce with increasingly weak literacy, math and science abilities.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-16">
  College graduates will still fare better than those with only a high school education, of course.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-17">
  But a university degree unaccompanied by a gain in knowledge or skills is an empty achievement indeed.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-18">
  For students who have been coasting through college, and for American universities that have been demanding less work, offering more attractions and charging higher tuition, the party may soon be over.
</SentenceNote>
