### Passage One · Should doctors consider the cost of care?

<SentenceNote number="R1-01">
  Saying they can no longer ignore the rising price of health care, some of the most influential medical groups in the nation are recommending that doctors weigh the costs, not just the effectiveness of treatments, as they make decisions about patient care.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-02">
  The shift, little noticed outside the medical establishment but already controversial inside it, suggests that doctors are starting to redefine their roles, from being concerned exclusively about individual patients to exerting influence on how healthcare dollars are spent.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-03">
  In practical terms, the new guidelines being developed could result in doctors choosing one drug over another for cost reasons or even deciding that a particular treatment—at the end of life, for example—is too expensive.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-04">
  In the extreme, some critics have said that making treatment decisions based on cost is a form of rationing.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-05">
  Traditionally, guidelines have heavily influenced the practice of medicine, and the latest ones are expected to make doctors more conscious of the economic consequences of their decisions, even though there’s no obligation to follow them.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-06">
  Medical society guidelines are also used by insurance companies to help determine reimbursement policies.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-07">
  Some doctors see a potential conflict in trying to be both providers of patient care and financial overseers.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-08">
  “There should be forces in society who should be concerned about the budget, but they shouldn’t be functioning simultaneously as doctors,” said Dr. Martin Samuels at a Boston hospital.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-09">
  He said doctors risked losing the trust of patients if they told patients, “I’m not going to do what I think is best for you because I think it’s bad for the healthcare budget in Massachusetts.”
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-10">
  Doctors can face some grim trade-offs.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-11">
  Studies have shown, for example, that two drugs are about equally effective in treating macular degeneration, an eye disease.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-12">
  But one costs \$50 a dose and the other close to \$2,000.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-13">
  Medicare could save hundreds of millions of dollars a year if everyone used the cheaper drug, Avastin, instead of the costlier one, Lucentis.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-14">

<SentenceNote number="R1-15">

</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R1-16">

</SentenceNote>

</SentenceNote>

### Passage Two · What really predicts upward mobility?

<SentenceNote number="R2-01">
  Economic inequality is the “defining challenge of our time,” President Barack Obama declared in a speech last month to the Center for American Progress.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-02">
  Inequality is dangerous, he argued, not merely because it doesn’t look good to have a large gap between the rich and the poor, but because inequality itself destroys upward mobility, making it harder for the poor to escape from poverty.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-03">
  “Increased inequality and decreasing mobility pose a fundamental threat to the American Dream,” he said.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-04">
  Obama is only the most prominent public figure to declare inequality Public Enemy No. 1 and the greatest threat to reducing poverty in America.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-05">
  A number of prominent economists have also argued that it’s harder for the poor to climb the economic ladder today because the rungs in that ladder have grown farther apart.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-06">
  For all the new attention devoted to the 1 percent, a new dataset from the Equality of Opportunity Project at Harvard and Berkeley suggests that, if we care about upward mobility overall, we’re vastly exaggerating the dangers of the rich-poor gap.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-07">
  Inequality itself is not a particularly strong predictor of economic mobility, as sociologist Scott Winship noted in a recent article based on his analysis of this data.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-08">
  So what factors, at the community level, do predict if poor children will move up the economic ladder as adults?
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-09">
  What explains, for instance, why the Salt Lake City metro area is one of the 100 largest metropolitan areas most likely to lift the fortunes of the poor and the Atlanta metro area is one of the least likely?
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-10">
  Harvard economist Raj Chetty has pointed to economic and racial segregation, community density, the size of a community’s middle class, the quality of schools, community religiosity, and family structure, which he calls the “single strongest correlate of upward mobility.”
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-11">
  Chetty finds that communities like Salt Lake City, with high levels of two-parent families and religiosity, are much more likely to see poor children get ahead than communities like Atlanta, with high levels of racial and economic segregation.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-12">
  Chetty has not yet issued a comprehensive analysis of the relative predictive power of each of these factors.
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-13">
  Based on my analyses of the data, of the factors that Chetty has highlighted, the following three seem to be most predictive of upward mobility in a given community:
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-14">
  1. Per-capita income growth
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-15">
  2. Prevalence of single mothers (where correlation is strong, but negative)
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-16">
  3. Per-capita local government spending
</SentenceNote>

<SentenceNote number="R2-17">
  In other words, communities with high levels of per-capita income growth, high percentages of two-parent families, and high local government spending—which may stand for good schools—are the most likely to help poor children relive the Horatio Alger’s rags-to-riches story.
</SentenceNote>
